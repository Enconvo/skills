When a strategy survives contact with reality.

Most strategies, written on a Tuesday, do not survive the next Monday's standup. The ones that do share a quiet trait: they were argued for, not announced. This is an inquiry into what happens when an organization treats its own plan as a hypothesis, and the market as the reviewer.

I
The Problem · §I

The plan is not the strategy.

Every quarter, somewhere in the building, a document is produced. It has slides. It has arrows. It has a section called "the strategy" and another called "execution," and these two sections — sealed off from each other by a section break — are precisely the problem. A plan describes the route. A strategy describes the theory of why the route works. When the theory is missing, the route is decoration.

We studied fourteen firms over the past decade, across financial services, industrial manufacturing, and consumer technology. The pattern repeated. Plans were written, presented, applauded. Within ninety days, the gap between document and behavior was wide enough to walk through. Not because the plan was wrong — but because the plan was never an argument that could be tested.

A plan is a contract with the future. A strategy is a wager on which future arrives first.
— Field note, fourth interview, June 2025

The firms that broke the pattern did one thing differently: they treated their own plan as a hypothesis. They named the conditions under which it would fail. They scheduled the reviews of those conditions before they scheduled the celebration of the plan itself. This is not paranoia. It is the simple discipline of writing down what you don't yet know.

A note from the editors — the firms that performed this exercise once, ceremonially, did not improve. The firms that performed it quarterly, with the same seriousness as the budget, did.

II
The Mechanism · §II

An argued strategy outlives an announced one.

Consider the difference between a strategy presented and a strategy defended. The first travels through the organization as a memo; the second travels as a debate. Memos are filed. Debates are remembered. Six months after an executive offsite, your people will not recall the slides — they will recall the questions that did not have answers, and what was eventually said when the silence broke.

The most useful artifact from a strategy review is not the deck. It is the list of objections that survived the room.
— Director of Strategy, Industrial Manufacturer, anonymized

In our case work, the firms that produced durable strategy did three things in tension with conventional practice. They invited their sharpest internal critics to write the counter-memo before the strategy was approved. They retained those counter-memos in the planning archive. And, twelve months later, they read them aloud — not to embarrass anyone, but to ask the only question that matters: which of these did we answer, and which are we still pretending we have?

III
The Practice · §III

What the discipline looks like on a Tuesday.

None of the above is exotic. It costs nothing beyond a willingness to be wrong on the record. The instrument is simple: a single page, written by the strategist, that names the three things that must be true for the strategy to work, the three signals that would tell us they are not true, and the date on which we will check. We call it the premise sheet. It belongs at the front of every plan and at the front of every review.

Organizations that adopted the premise sheet did not produce more strategies. They produced fewer. The strategies they produced, however, were continuous — they survived the contact with the market because they had been built, from the first sentence, to anticipate it.

End of the argument
The Idea in Brief

A strategy survives the market when it was already argued with in the room.

i.
The Idea

Plans describe routes. Strategies describe why those routes are the right ones. The second is a hypothesis — and like all hypotheses, it must be testable, or it is decoration.

ii.
The Evidence

Across fourteen firms over a decade, the strategies that survived were the ones defended against named, written objections — not the ones that were merely approved.

iii.
The Practice

Adopt the premise sheet: name the three things that must be true, the three signals that would tell you they aren't, and the date you will check. Keep it at the front of the plan. Read it again.

Four Takeaways

What to do, on Monday.

i.

Write the premise sheet first.

Before the slides, name the three propositions on which the strategy rests. If you cannot, you do not yet have a strategy — you have a wish list with a budget line attached.

ii.

Invite the counter-memo into the archive.

Ask your sharpest critic to write a one-page argument against the plan. File it next to the plan. Re-read both before any review. Memory will not do this work for you.

iii.

Schedule the disconfirmation date.

Plans rarely fail at announcement. They fail at the silence that follows. Put the date the world will tell you something on the calendar, before you celebrate the launch.

iv.

Reread the list of objections.

Twelve months in, do not ask "are we on track." Ask "which of the objections from the room have we actually answered, and which are we still pretending we have?"
